# Why Do Onions Make Us Cry?

## The strange alarm hidden in a kitchen staple

An onion can sit quietly on a counter for days and do nothing to your eyes.
Cut it open, and within seconds the same vegetable can make you blink, sting, and tear up.
That feels almost absurd.
Nothing has been heated, burned, or sprayed.
You simply broke a few layers of plant tissue.
But that damage is exactly the point.
An onion does not store its irritating chemistry as one ready-made gas waiting to escape.
It stores separate ingredients inside living cells, and cutting lets those ingredients meet.
What happens next is a tiny chemical chain reaction built into the plant.
And the tears are not an emotional response at all.
They are a protective reflex from your eyes.

## The onion keeps the ingredients apart

Inside an intact onion, sulfur-containing precursor compounds and an enzyme called alliinase are kept in different parts of the cells.
Alliinase is simply a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction.
As long as the cells stay intact, the ingredients are largely separated.
A knife changes that arrangement instantly.
When the blade breaks cell walls and membranes, compounds that were stored apart spill into the same damaged tissue.
Alliinase can now act on the sulfur-containing precursors.
That reaction produces unstable sulfur compounds called sulfenic acids.
They do not remain in that form for long.
They are reactive intermediates, temporary molecules created on the way to other products.
This is why chopping an onion changes its smell almost immediately.
The odor is not just something that was trapped inside the bulb.
Part of it is being manufactured at the moment the tissue is damaged.

## The enzyme scientists did not expect

For years, researchers thought the tear-causing molecule formed more or less automatically after alliinase began the reaction.
Then a 2002 Nature paper changed the picture.
The researchers showed that onions use another enzyme, lachrymatory-factor synthase, to specifically make the tear-causing factor.
The name sounds intimidating, but its job is simple: it helps turn a short-lived sulfur intermediate into the molecule that makes your eyes water.
That finding matters because the onion is not merely releasing a random irritating by-product.
It has a dedicated biochemical step that produces it.
Later structural and biochemical work examined how this enzyme performs that conversion.
The chemistry is fast enough that, from a cook's point of view, it seems instantaneous.
Slice, and the reaction begins.

## The molecule that reaches your eyes

The main tear-causing compound is syn-propanethial S-oxide, a small volatile sulfur compound often called the onion lachrymatory factor.
Volatile means it can readily enter the air.
Once it forms in the damaged onion, some of it moves away from the cutting board and reaches the moist surface of your eyes.
There it acts as an irritant.
Your eye does not need to know that the molecule came from a vegetable.
It only needs to detect that the surface is being chemically irritated.
That triggers reflex tears, the protective tears produced when dust, smoke, or another irritant reaches the eye.
The extra fluid helps dilute and wash away the irritating material.
So the stream running down your face is not the onion somehow forcing emotion out of you.
It is your eye performing a cleanup response.

## Why onions evolved such chemistry

The reaction makes more sense when we stop thinking like cooks and start thinking like plants.
An onion cannot run away when something bites into it.
Chemical defense is one of the tools available to a plant that is rooted in place.
Researchers studying onion chemistry describe the sulfur pathway as part of a broader defense system against microbes and animals.
Damage activates a burst of reactive sulfur chemistry.
Some of the products contribute to the sharp smell and flavor we associate with onions.
One branch of that chemistry produces the lachrymatory factor.
From the plant's perspective, tissue damage is a moment when chemical defense is useful.
From our perspective, it is the moment dinner becomes uncomfortable.
The same chemistry can therefore be both biologically useful to the plant and culinarily annoying to us.

## Why some cuts feel worse than others

The amount of irritation you experience can vary because several things change how much reactive chemistry is created and how much of the volatile product reaches your face.
More tissue damage means more cell contents can mix.
Air movement matters because the tear factor has to travel from the onion to your eyes.
The onion itself also matters.
Different onions do not contain identical amounts of the precursor compounds or produce identical levels of pungent sulfur chemistry.
And people differ in how strongly they react to irritants.
That is why two people can chop the same onion and report very different experiences.
The underlying pathway can be the same even when the subjective intensity is not.

## Can an onion be made that does not make you cry?

Yes, at least in experimental lines, researchers have shown that the pathway can be redirected.
Studies that suppressed the activity of lachrymatory-factor synthase produced onions with dramatically reduced tear-factor formation.
When that enzyme is less active, the sulfur intermediate is available for other reactions instead of being efficiently converted into the lachrymatory factor.
That result is useful for two reasons.
First, it confirms that the tear-producing enzyme really is a key control point in the pathway.
Second, it shows that reducing tears does not require removing all sulfur chemistry from the onion.
The plant's chemistry can be shifted rather than simply switched off.
Breeding and biotechnology can therefore change how strongly an onion activates this particular defense branch.

## What actually helps in the kitchen

Once you understand the mechanism, many kitchen tricks become easier to judge.
The tear factor has to be produced in damaged tissue and then reach your eyes through the air.
So the most direct strategy is to reduce your exposure to the airborne irritant.
Moving air away from your face with good ventilation can help carry the volatile compound elsewhere.
A very sharp knife can also reduce unnecessary crushing compared with a badly blunted blade, although cutting still damages cells and cannot stop the chemistry completely.
Anything that places your eyes farther from the rising vapor can reduce exposure simply by changing where the air goes.
The important point is that no trick needs to "neutralize emotion" or pull toxins out of the onion.
This is ordinary plant chemistry meeting ordinary eye protection.

## The whole chain in one cut

So why do onions make us cry?
Because a knife turns separated cellular ingredients into a rapid chemical assembly line.
Cell damage brings sulfur precursors together with alliinase.
Alliinase creates short-lived sulfur intermediates.
Lachrymatory-factor synthase redirects one of those intermediates toward syn-propanethial S-oxide.
That volatile molecule reaches the eye and irritates its surface.
The eye answers with reflex tears that dilute and wash away the irritant.
The remarkable part is how much biology is compressed into those few seconds.
What looks like a simple nuisance in the kitchen is actually a defense system, an enzyme pathway, an airborne chemical signal, and a protective human reflex unfolding one after another.
An onion does not contain a tiny bottle of tear gas.
It contains the machinery to make an irritant when its tissue is damaged.
Your knife starts the chemistry.
Your eyes finish the story.
